WELCOME TO SURF CITY

The One Rule

Alright, partners—welcome to Huntington Beach, where the water is salty, the fish can be picky, the wind will slap your hat sideways, and everybody on the pier has a fishing story that gets bigger than a bluefin by sunset.

Whether you are soaking bait in the surf, walking a jetty like a mountain goat with a tackle bag, casting from the Huntington Beach Pier, or climbing aboard a charter boat out of Newport Beach, Long Beach, or San Pedro, there is one rule above all others:

Catch fish if you can—but make it home with the same number of holes you left with.
SHORE FISHING

Watch Your Step, Cowboy

Surf fishing around Huntington Beach can put you on barred surfperch, croaker, corbina, halibut, and whatever else is hungry enough to grab your bait. But the Pacific is not a swimming pool, and there are a few little troublemakers hiding beneath that sand.

RED FLAG: STINGRAYS IN THE SHALLOWS

When you step into the surf, shuffle your feet, cowboy—do not stomp around like you are trying to start a dance-off with the Pacific. Those little sand pancakes are usually hiding out and minding their own business, but step on one and they may turn your fishing day into a one-foot limping contest.

Slide your feet through the sand rather than taking regular steps, giving stingrays a chance to scoot away. Stingrays typically sting only in defense when someone accidentally steps on them.

Unless you are still wearing boots in the surf—then shuffle those boots too, partner. But most folks ought to leave the cowboy boots up on dry land and do the stingray shuffle barefoot or in water shoes.

SHORE FISHING

Never Turn Your Back on the Ocean

RED FLAG: SHOREBREAK AND SNEAKER WAVES

Never turn your back on the ocean, partner. A calm-looking set can turn mean in a hurry, and one hard shorebreak wave can knock your boots out from under you faster than a gull can steal a breakfast burrito.

Before you fish, watch the water for a few minutes. Pay attention to the swell, current, tide, and where waves are breaking. Keep your tackle, cooler, and phone above the high-water line unless you want your gear headed for Catalina without you.

RED FLAG: RIP CURRENTS

Rip currents can form at any beach and are especially common around piers, jetties, reefs, and groins. If you get caught in one, stay calm and do not try to fight straight back toward shore. Move parallel to the beach if you can, float to conserve energy, and signal for help.

BEACH GEAR, RIGS & BAIT

Keep It Simple, Keep It Moving

For most Huntington Beach surf fishing, a 9- to 12-foot surf rod helps get your bait beyond the break. A Carolina rig is one of the best all-around setups for local beach fishing.

GOOD BAIT
Sand crabsBloodwormsClamsMusselsArtificial grubsCut squid
COMMON SURF TARGETS
Barred surfperchCalifornia corbinaYellowfin croakerSpotfin croakerCalifornia halibut

Early morning, late afternoon, and a moving or incoming tide can be good times to fish. Keep moving down the beach and look for darker water, troughs, bait schools, birds, holes, and current seams. Do not stand in one spot all day like a fence post if the fish are not there.

And remember: keep clear of swimmers, surfers, volleyball crowds, and anybody enjoying the beach. A Carolina rig might catch fish, but it is a terrible way to make friends if it lands near somebody’s beach towel.

JETTY FISHING

Slick Rocks and Salty Goblins

Fishing jetties, rock structures, and harbor entrances can be productive, but those rocks are slick, uneven, sharp, and unforgiving. One bad step can put you in the drink faster than you can say, “I thought that rock was dry.”

RED FLAG: JETTY ROCKS ARE SLICKER THAN A USED-CAR SALES PITCH
  • Wear shoes or boots with serious traction—cleated footwear or proper rock-fishing footwear, not flip-flops.
  • Flip-flops are for walking to the taco stand—not hopping around wet ocean rocks.
  • Wear a properly fitted life jacket around exposed rocks, jetties, or breakwaters.
  • Do not fish a jetty when swell is large, wind is strong, rocks are being washed over, or visibility is poor.
  • Keep your hands free while moving; pack gear so you can climb carefully.
  • Carry a headlamp if you fish around sunrise, sunset, or after dark.
  • Tell somebody where you are going and when you expect to be back.
JETTY FISHING

Feathered Tax Collectors

The jetties and harbor mouths have their own collection of local wildlife: pelicans, cormorants, sea lions, gulls, crabs, mussels, baitfish, and sometimes a seal popping up like he bought himself a front-row ticket.

Keep bait, food, and snacks covered. The gulls are not your friends. They are feathered tax collectors.

Those harbor gulls will steal your sandwich, look you right in the eye, and still act like you owe them rent.
FISH THE STRUCTURE

Spotted bay bass, calico bass, halibut, opaleye, perch, and croaker may hold near rocks, current seams, kelp, pilings, and tide-fed pockets. Be ready for snags—that is the price you pay for fishing where the fish live.

PIER FISHING

Surf City's Living Room

The Huntington Beach Pier is more than a fishing spot—it is a front-row seat to the whole Surf City experience. You have surfers, cyclists, walkers, musicians, beach volleyball, families, photographers, food smells drifting in from Main Street, and at least one person telling you the fish were biting “way better yesterday.”

It is never boring out there.

RED FLAG: LOOK BEHIND YOU BEFORE CASTING
  • Before every cast, look behind you. Then look again.
  • Watch for kids, pedestrians, other anglers, bikes, and people walking into your casting lane.
  • Avoid overhead casting in crowded areas.
  • Keep loose hooks, knives, bait, and tackle contained.
  • Do not lean over, climb, or sit on railings.
  • Be aware of anglers above or below you.
  • Use a hoop net or landing device for larger fish instead of swinging a big fish over the rail.
PIER FISHING

License, Rules & the Lineup

A qualifying public pier such as Huntington Beach Pier generally allows anglers to fish without a California fishing license. However, California bag limits, size limits, seasonal closures, species rules, and other fishing regulations still apply. Do not confuse “no license required on the pier” with “no rules required.”

For shore fishing, jetty fishing, kayak fishing, or charter-boat fishing, anglers age 16 and older generally need a valid California fishing license. Always check current regulations for the species and location you plan to fish.

RED FLAG: RESPECT THE SURF LINEUP

This is Surf City—not “Cast Into the Lineup City.”

Keep your lines, sinkers, hooks, and casts clear of swimmers and surfers. Baja Troy surfs too, and a fisherman tangling a surfer is a quick way to turn a peaceful fishing day into a pier-side town hall meeting.

Respect the lineup. If surfers are thick in an area, move down the beach, change your casting angle, fish another section of the pier, or return at a quieter time.

FIELD GUIDE ENTRY

The Pier Land Shark

And now, a special wildlife entry from the Baja Troy field guide:

Land shark: Usually a young single fella prowling the pier or beach with maximum confidence, minimum tackle, and a radar system permanently locked onto the ladies.

You can identify land sharks by their behavior. They travel in packs, use a lot of cologne, carry a fishing rod with no bait on it, and somehow spend four hours at the pier without ever once checking their line.

Remember, partner: the land shark is catch-and-release only. Do not feed it attention after midnight.
OFFSHORE CHARTER FISHING

Let the Saltwater Pros Run the Show

Booking a charter from Newport Beach, Long Beach, San Pedro, or nearby landings is a great way to get offshore and target larger or different species than you will usually find from shore.

You are not just buying a boat ride—you are getting a captain, deckhands, bait, boat handling, safety knowledge, local fishing information, and a crew that can tell the difference between “the fish are biting” and “somebody is about to get green around the gills.”

The captain and deckhands have sea legs. Most of them were born with salt crust on their boots and can walk across a rolling deck carrying three rods, a gaff, and a cup of coffee without spilling a drop.

You, on the other hand, may discover your sea legs have not been issued yet.
OFFSHORE CHARTER FISHING

Before You Book, Ask These

Shop around. There are many charter operations and landings in Newport Beach, Long Beach, and San Pedro, and not every boat offers the same trip, target species, schedule, gear, or amenities.

  • What species are being targeted right now?
  • Half-day, three-quarter-day, full-day, overnight, or longer?
  • Are rods, reels, tackle, bait, and hooks included?
  • Does the boat have a galley, food, drinks, coffee, or snacks?
  • Is fish cleaning or fillet service available?
  • What time is check-in, and where do I park?
  • Do I need my own fishing license or any special report card?
  • What should I bring for weather, warmth, and motion sickness?
OFFSHORE CHARTER FISHING

Seasickness Doesn't Care How Tough You Are

RED FLAG: THE CHUM LINE DONATION

If you know you get seasick, do not wait until you are leaning over the rail and negotiating with the ocean.

Take appropriate motion-sickness medicine ahead of time if needed, following the label instructions or guidance from a pharmacist or clinician. Eat a light meal before the trip, stay hydrated, get fresh air, look at the horizon, and avoid staring down at your phone during the boat ride.

The ocean will humble a man quick. One minute you are talking about yellowtail limits, and the next minute you are making a personal donation to the chum line.
LISTEN TO THE CREW

When you board, listen to the captain’s safety briefing—even if you have been on plenty of boats before. Know where the life jackets are, how to move safely around the deck, which areas are off-limits, where to stand while fishing, how the crew wants fish handled, and what to do during rough weather, tangles, hook-ups, or emergencies.

Use handrails and brace yourself when the boat is moving. Do not walk around holding rods, knives, hot coffee, and three tacos like you are auditioning for the circus.

OFFSHORE CHARTER FISHING

Tackle & Targets

For general half-day or three-quarter-day Southern California trips, a 20- to 30-pound conventional or heavy spinning setup is common. Live anchovies and sardines work well on sliding-sinker and dropper-loop rigs, while surface iron can be a lot of fun for barracuda and bonito when conditions are right.

WHAT YOU MIGHT TARGET
Calico bassSand bassRockfishBarracudaBonitoYellowtailHalibutWhite seabassTuna (longer trips)

It all depends on the season, location, water temperature, and the current bite—which is exactly why you ask the crew what’s working before you drop a line.

CLOTHING & LAYERS

Dress for the Wind’s Personal Grudge

Southern California weather will lie to you. It may look warm from the parking lot, then you get out on the pier or boat and the coastal wind starts cutting through your shirt like it has a personal grudge.

DRESS IN LAYERS
  • Lightweight long-sleeve UV shirt
  • Hoodie, windbreaker, or light waterproof jacket
  • Closed-toe shoes or boots with good traction
  • Wide-brim hat or ball cap with a retainer
  • Polarized sunglasses with a leash
  • Extra socks for wet sand, boats, or rock fishing

Polarized sunglasses are not just for looking cool, although they do help with that too. They reduce glare and can help you spot troughs, sandbars, structure, bait, and fish in shallow water.

SUN & WEATHER

The Sun Owns the Coastline

Use broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher. Apply it before heading out and reapply at least every two hours, or sooner after sweating or getting wet. Do not forget your ears, neck, nose, hands, calves, the back of your knees, and the top of your feet.

If you come home looking like a boiled lobster, that is not a fishing tan. That is the sun reminding you who owns the coastline.

Check the surf-zone forecast, weather, wind, swell, tides, and beach advisories before you leave. The National Weather Service warns that life-threatening rip currents can occur near jetties, reefs, groins, and piers.

BAJA TROY'S CHECKLIST

The Local Gear Checklist

Bring the basics, even if you are “only going for an hour.” Those are famous last words in fishing.

  • Valid California fishing license when required
  • Current California ocean sport-fishing regulations
  • Rod, reel, line, leader, hooks, sinkers, lures, and bait
  • Long needle-nose pliers or hook-removal tool
  • Small first-aid kit
  • Measuring device for legal fish
  • Cooler and ice if you plan to keep fish
  • Water and snacks
  • Sunscreen, hat, and polarized sunglasses
  • Trash bag for used line, bait packaging, and garbage
  • Life jacket for exposed rocks, small boats, or jetties
  • Headlamp for early, late, or nighttime fishing
  • Phone in a waterproof pouch

Always check current California regulations before keeping fish—size limits, bag limits, seasonal closures, Marine Protected Areas, depth restrictions, and report-card rules can change depending on where and what you fish.

TAKE A BREAK

Main Street Is Right There

One of the best things about fishing the Huntington Beach Pier is that you are right at the end of Main Street. When the fish get tight-lipped, the bait runs low, or you need to stretch your legs, there is plenty to do just a few steps inland.

Main Street has a lively lineup of restaurants, coffee stops, bars, shopping, entertainment, and first-class people-watching. Whether you want a quick bite after a morning on the pier, a cold drink after surf fishing, or somewhere to relax after a charter trip, it is all close by.

Baja Troy’s Local Tip: When the fish are not cooperating, do not sit there arguing with your bait. Walk up Main Street, fuel up, enjoy the action, and come back with a better attitude—and maybe a better fishing story.

BAJA TROY’S TAKE

Still Classic, But Not What It Used to Be

Huntington Beach is still a classic Surf City place to fish. You have the pier, the shoreline, the jetties, surfers in the water, plenty of action around you, and Main Street right there when the fish get tight-lipped and you need a bite to eat instead.

It is scenic, easy to get to, fun with friends or family, and there is almost always something going on. You can fish, watch the surf, enjoy the ocean, and still have a good day even when the bite is slow.

But I am going to give it to you straight, cowboy: the fishing is not what it used to be.

When I was younger, I caught more variety from the pier, shore, and jetties. After spending a lot of time fishing this area over the years, I have watched it get more pressured and less consistent. You can still catch fish out there—but it is no longer the kind of place where you just show up, cast out, and expect the cooler to get heavy.

BAJA TROY’S TAKE · PART 2

Work For Your Bite

The pier, beach, and jetties can still produce surfperch, croaker, corbina, mackerel, bass, halibut, and a few surprises when conditions line up. But nowadays it takes patience, the right tide, the right bait, moving around, and maybe a little blessing from the fish gods.

“Some days Huntington Beach will hand you a fish story. Other days it will hand you seaweed, a sunburn, and a gull that stole your sandwich.”
BAJA TROY’S TAKE · PART 3

Catch, Release & Keep the Tradition Alive

For me personally, this is a catch-and-release spot. That is just Baja Troy’s style for this particular place—not a lecture and not me telling anybody else what to do with a legal fish.

If you catch a legal fish and want to keep it for dinner, that is your call, partner. But with the amount of fishing pressure on the pier, beach, and jetties, I personally like seeing a lot of those local fish swim back out, get bigger, and give somebody else a chance down the trail.

“Keep what is legal and what you will use. Let the rest swim another day. That is not preaching, cowboy—that is just Baja Troy’s style.”

I do not want these local fishing spots to become one of those “back in the day” stories—where everybody talks about how good it used to be because nobody thought about tomorrow while they were fishing today.

If you are looking for a good time, ocean views, a little Surf City atmosphere, and a chance to bend a rod, Huntington Beach is still worth it. If you are looking for stronger and more consistent fishing, the charter boats running out of Newport, Long Beach, and San Pedro are usually the better move.

“The good fishing may be farther offshore, partner—but Huntington Beach is still a good place to cast a line, clear your head, and enjoy the salt air.”
FINAL REMINDER

Fish Hard, Laugh Often

Fish hard, laugh often, respect the ocean, respect the surfers, respect the crew, and respect the fish.

Shuffle your feet for stingrays. Watch the swell. Keep your hooks away from people. Do not trust a gull. Do not argue with a sea lion. And if the bite is slow, remember:

A bad day fishing in Huntington Beach still beats a good day sitting inside wishing you were fishing.
Tight lines, dry boots, and may your cooler be heavier on the walk back than it was on the walk out.
