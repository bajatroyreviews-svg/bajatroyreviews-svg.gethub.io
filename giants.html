Intro — among the real giants

Out here above the Kern, the air gets thin, the road gets twisty, and the world gets quiet. You leave the city, the traffic, the bumper-to-bumper life, and you climb into a place where the only giants that matter are the ones that’ve been standin’ here for fifteen hundred years.

The Trail of 100 Giants in Long Meadow Grove ain’t just a walk — it’s a church service without walls. These big trees don’t care about your job title, your debt, or your damn to-do list. They care about one thing: remindin’ you that you’re small, that you’re blessed, and that you got one more day to share with friends, family, and the good earth.

I first walked this trail with a couple of buddies from England. We pulled up, shut our mouths, and just stared. Ain’t no talk big enough for that. We were in awe, plain and simple. Ever since, every time I make that climb, I’m reminded: the most important things in life ain’t things at all. They’re people, they’re nature, and they’re moments where you feel grounded, humbled, and grateful just to be breathin’.

The basics — Trail of 100 Giants

What it is: a paved, wheelchair-accessible loop trail (about 1.3 miles) through one of the premier giant sequoia groves in the Sierra Nevada

Located in Long Meadow Grove, part of the Giant Sequoia National Monument in Sequoia National Forest

Region & nearest town

Nearest town: Kernville, CA — your last real stop for gas, food, and supplies

Nearest bigger city: Bakersfield, CA — about 1.5–2 hours down the mountain

Distance & drive time (approx.)

  • From Kernville — ~45 minutes to 1 hour, depending on road conditions and how often you stop to gawk
  • From Bakersfield — ~2 to 2.5 hours via Highway 178 to Kernville, then up to the Western Divide Highway
  • From Los Angeles — ~3 to 4 hours, usually via I-5 to Highway 178

Elevation & terrain

  • Elevation: roughly 6,000–6,500 feet in the grove
  • Terrain: paved loop trail, gentle grades; side trails into the grove are dirt and needle-covered
  • Accessibility: wheelchair- and stroller-friendly on the main loop; side trails can be rougher

Access, fees & permits

Access & fees

  • Day use fee: typically around $5–$10 per vehicle — check current Forest Service rates
  • Payment: self-pay station at the trailhead — bring cash just in case
  • Season: usually late May through November, weather and road-closure dependent; winter snow can close the road

Permits

  • Day use / hiking: no wilderness permit required just to walk the trail
  • Developed campground: pay the campground fee and follow their rules
  • Dispersed camping with a stove, campfire, or grill: free California Campfire Permit (get it online via Cal Fire)
  • Some areas have fire restrictions or no-camping zones, especially in high fire danger season

Camping nearby & supplies

Camping nearby

  • Developed campgrounds around Lake Isabella, Kernville, and along the Western Divide Highway (check current status) — typically $20–$35/night
  • Dispersed camping: often allowed in parts of Sequoia National Forest, but not everywhere — use established pull-outs, never day-use areas
  • Leave No Trace: pack out trash, don’t damage trees, respect fire rules

Nearest gas, food & supplies

  • Kernville is your best bet — gas, groceries, restaurants, bait shops, outdoor stores. Fill up before heading up the mountain; stations get sparse above town
  • Lake Isabella / Wofford Heights: smaller selection; don’t count on it in peak season

Weather, seasons & what to expect

Best months: late May through October, when the road is open and the weather’s mild

  • Summer — warm days, cool nights; afternoon thunderstorms possible
  • Fall — crisp air, fewer crowds, beautiful light
  • Spring — snowmelt, muddy patches, some road sections may be rough
  • Winter — snow, ice, road closures; not a winter day-hike unless you’re prepared for backcountry conditions

What to wear & bring

  • Sturdy shoes — you’ll step off onto needles and dirt
  • Layers — mornings and evenings run cool, even in summer
  • Hat, sunglasses, sunscreen
  • Water: 1–2 liters per person; more if it’s hot
  • Snacks: trail mix, fruit, bars
  • Camera — you’ll want one
  • Trekking poles (optional): nice for balance if you’re unsteady or have injuries

Facilities — restrooms, parking, accessibility

  • Vault toilets at the trailhead / parking area — no flush toilets or running water on site; bring hand sanitizer
  • Paved parking lot; fills up on summer weekends — arrive early
  • Main loop is paved and wheelchair-accessible
  • Side trails are dirt and needle-covered, rougher for chairs and strollers
  • Benches along the way to rest and soak it in

The hike — walking among giants

Trail length & difficulty

1.3-mile loop, easy grade, well-maintained — suitable for all ages and fitness levels. Allow 45 minutes to 1.5 hours, depending on how often you stop to stare, pray, or just breathe.

What you’ll see

  • Hundreds of giant sequoias — 125+ trees over 10 feet in diameter
  • Some over 200 feet tall and 20 feet across
  • Interpretive signs on the ecology, fire history, and cultural significance
  • The famous “Fallen Monarchs” and other trees that lived through centuries of fire, drought, and change

The hike — Baja Troy style

How to walk it

  • Start slow. Let the city noise fall off your shoulders like dead bark.
  • Stop at the big ones. Put your hand on the trunk. Breathe.
  • Think about your friends, your family, your loved ones — the ones who put up with your stories and your long weekends.
  • Remember: these trees don’t rush. They don’t compete. They just stand, grow, and endure.
  • If you feel your eyes water, let ’em. Ain’t no shame in bein’ moved.

Safety, limitations & common sense

Safety first

  • Stay on designated trails; don’t climb on or carve into the trees
  • Watch for ice in spring and fall — the pavement can slick up
  • Summer thunderstorms: if you hear thunder, start headin’ back
  • Wildlife — deer, squirrels, birds: give ’em space

Know your limitations

  • Injuries, mobility issues, or a body that tires easy? Take it slow
  • Use benches. Sit. Rest. There’s no prize for rushin’ through a cathedral
  • Cane, walker, or wheelchair — the main loop is your friend

Leave No Trace

  • Pack out all trash
  • Don’t pick plants or disturb the forest floor
  • Respect quiet — this place heals folks in ways you can’t see

Gear & prep — don’t get caught off guard

  • Footwear: hiking shoes or sturdy sneakers — flip-flops will get humbled by the needles
  • Clothing: moisture-wicking base layer; light jacket or fleece for mornings and evenings; rain shell if storms are forecast
  • Water: 1–2 liters minimum per person; snacks that travel well
  • Phone with offline maps — service is spotty; print or screenshot Forest Service info on road conditions and fees
  • Check current status before you go: Sequoia National Forest website, Giant Sequoia National Monument pages, local ranger district for fire restrictions and closures

Optional but nice

  • Trekking poles for balance
  • Small backpack with first-aid kit, hand sanitizer, extra layer
  • Camera or phone with extra battery

Camping options nearby

  • Around Kernville and Lake Isabella: drive-in campgrounds with tables, fire rings, vault toilets — typically $20–$35/night; check status and reservations
  • Along the Western Divide Highway: more remote, often first-come first-served; vault toilets, no hookups
  • Dispersed: allowed in many parts of the forest, not in day-use areas or next to the trail; use established sites; Campfire Permit required for stove, fire, or grill; some years fires are banned completely

For single moms & families

  • Developed campgrounds are easier: real tables, defined sites, other families around
  • Dispersed is more rustic: no neighbors, no facilities, more responsibility
  • Either way — tell someone where you’re going, when you expect back, and what vehicle you’re in

The soul stuff — why this place matters

Out here, you’re not fightin’ traffic. You’re not answerin’ emails. You’re not carryin’ the weight of bills, politics, or other folks’ expectations. You’re standin’ among giants that don’t give a damn about your résumé.

These trees’ve seen fire, drought, loggers, tourists, and time itself. They’ve lost limbs, scarred over, and kept growin’. They don’t brag. They don’t boast. They just stand.

This ain’t snake oil. This is nature’s fix. Nature’s bubbly. The real deal. You show up with your noise, your rush, your worry — and you leave lighter. Not ’cause the trees fixed you, but ’cause they reminded you what’s real.

If you’re carryin’ a heavy week, a loud mind, or a heart that’s been beat on — this place’ll meet you where you are. It won’t fix you. But it’ll remind you that you’re part of somethin’ bigger, older, and stronger than your worst day.

Bring your friends. Bring your family. Bring your loved ones. Or bring your quiet self and let the trees do the talkin’. Just make the trip. Do the homework. Get the right gear. Respect the rules. And let these giants humble you.
